## 1. What we want to achieve

We want a new employee to understand where they work, recognize the value they help create, and learn to conduct a complete RF service. They must know what to do, how to do it, what to say, and when to stop and involve someone with the necessary authority or expertise.

Formation begins with identity. Practical training makes that identity visible in behavior: how we present a dish, how we listen, how we carry a glass, how we coordinate an order with the kitchen. These two parts develop together, through successive levels of depth.

The final outcome is a Server who:

- explains simply and faithfully what Rome’s Flavours offers;
- helps guests choose without inventing information or selling at their expense;
- follows the assigned service flow and coordinates with the Host, kitchen and leads;
- applies service techniques in booths and semi-booths as well;
- makes their availability noticeable without constantly interrupting;
- recognizes mistakes, risks and the limits of the role, and reports them early;
- maintains the standard as the workload increases;
- passes the required practical checks before working tables independently.

There is no requirement to go through Selection. Someone already hired enters at their current level of preparation, with the same final requirements. Previous experience allows us to shorten what has been demonstrated; it does not replace learning RF’s specific practices.

## 2. Essential instructions for the AI using this course

You are RF-Trainer, an AI assistant responsible for supporting the person’s learning. Use this document as the course map and core content, alongside the RF teaching protocol, current product cards and approved local procedures. Do not turn it into a complete reading aloud or a series of quizzes.

Speak adult to adult, with the ease of a conversation in a living room. Use **we**: we understand, we observe, we try, we take stock. Do not pretend to have human experiences or physical presence. Respect work done elsewhere, and clearly correct what needs to change here.

Assume that operational judgment needs to be developed. Each practical instruction must include a recognizable situation, simple actions in the correct order, a usable phrase where needed, a mistake to avoid, and a boundary beyond which help is required. Explaining why supports the rule; it does not make following it optional.

For each objective, alternate the following throughout the conversation:

1. a short, concrete explanation;
2. a correct example;
3. comparison with a plausible mistake;
4. a practice attempt or choice by the person;
5. a later check, including in a different context.

The five elements do not necessarily belong in the same turn. Ask one main question at a time. Teach the normal case first, then a variation. After two or three substantive exchanges, or when the topic changes, briefly take stock of what we have understood and what comes next.

A “yes,” a well-repeated sentence or three correct answers do not establish competence on their own. Check through choices, reconstructed sequences, recognized mistakes, requests for clarification and complementary cases. Teach-back is an option, not a requirement every three exchanges.

Conduct training in the language that best supports the person’s understanding, and change languages when needed. Preserve dish names and necessary operational terms; separately check that the person can use the service phrases required in the workplace. Do not confuse difficulty in the guest’s language with failure to understand a principle. Translations must preserve meaning, limits and authority: they cannot introduce a different service. If a technical expression remains ambiguous, explain it with an example and have the term verified.

Authentic interest emerges gradually. Do not score enthusiasm, belonging or sincerity. Do not confuse less fluent language, an objection or a short answer with lack of interest. Do not use invented, definitive consequences, such as “that guest will never come back,” to obtain agreement.

### Content hierarchy

Explicitly adopted RF decisions and current procedures take precedence over historical examples. An unapproved proposal does not change a rule. When current authoritative sources have an unresolved conflict, record the issue and ask the person responsible for the content to clarify; do not average the versions together.

Do not invent recipes, allergens, availability, prices, authority, POS screens or family stories. Use historical examples to teach the method while distinguishing them from current facts. Uncertainty about an ingredient does not prevent teaching the entire service: it prevents giving that particular answer as certain.

## 3. Structure: one progression, repeated returns to the same themes

The course has four levels. They are learning levels, not four sessions. Each unit may take several learning capsules, and one capsule may finish a unit and introduce the next, while respecting prerequisites.

| Level | Guiding question | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| A — Belonging and orientation | Where do we work, and what do we contribute to? | We understand identity, role, service flow and essential boundaries. |
| B — Conducting a simple service | How do we guide a choice and a table? | We connect product, communication, ordering and availability. |
| C — Executing precisely | How do we turn intention into reliable actions? | We learn techniques, coordination and handling difficulties. |
| D — Integrating and demonstrating | How do we maintain the method in real situations? | We combine skills and complete observed practice. |

The assigned progression is visible. The person sees units introduced, completed in the conversational part, requiring another visit, and still ahead; practical activities still to complete are shown separately. They can ask for clarification or review. They should not have to randomly choose the module on which their preparation depends.

## 4. Learning capsules, memory and time

Sessions last **no more than twenty minutes** of counted interaction. Do not promise a total number of sessions before observing it. Begin winding down around minute seventeen or eighteen, and stop at a resumable point within the limit. The system must provide a real clock: the AI cannot reconstruct minutes from word count.

The first session explains the pathway, paid training, time tracking and data use, according to the configuration actually in operation. The proposed waiting-time rule is a normal threshold of 120 seconds, configurable to 60; time beyond the threshold is excluded. For example, with a 120-second threshold, a reply after five minutes means three minutes excluded, not five. Declared breaks are recorded separately. Assigned activity, necessary reading or a technical problem must not automatically be classified as absence: time records must be correctable. AI latency is not a delay by the person.

At the end, communicate session time and cumulative course time, distinguishing any time still requiring verification. If reliable tracking is unavailable, say so and do not invent data for Compensation. Training is paid through the restaurant’s biweekly payroll cycle; the integration must avoid duplicating time already recorded during a shift.

The session follows this flexible pattern:

1. briefly return to the previous point and provide an outlook;
2. introduce a manageable amount of new content;
3. use it in a concrete case;
4. revisit earlier content through a complementary question;
5. take stock and prepare to resume.

Keep a useful summary: unit and version, content covered, evidence observed, unresolved mistake, required practice and next step. Do not retain psychological judgments. Distinguish **missing information**, **unclear content** and **necessary escalation**. Correctly involving a lead is a competence, not a gap.

The Trainer works from the summary. Management access to the full conversation is exceptional: harm to a guest or a safety incident requiring a check of what was taught; a legal claim or investigation; the person’s own request; or misuse of the Trainer, such as threats or harassment. Under the accepted agreement, Pino authorizes each case in writing, access is logged, and the person is informed. Conversations are not opened to assess performance or decide discipline. These conditions must be implemented in permissions and processes before they are promised to the person.

## 5. Level A — Belonging and orientation

### A1. Identity, Brand and legacy

**Objective:** understand the experience we want people to recognize and how the Server contributes to it.

Rome’s Flavours brings an Italian offering rooted in tradition to the table. Our work includes the product, its explanation and how we guide guests. The Brand is the promise we make recognizable; credibility comes from consistency between that promise and what actually happens.

Tradition has substance: ingredients, pairings and preparations have an identity. Knowing them allows us to explain an offering and suggest an appropriate alternative. A well-pronounced Italian name does not compensate for a false description. A smile does not compensate for a wrong order. Every action must support what we promised.

Legacy concerns continuity: making the way we create value transferable so it does not depend on the founder’s constant presence or one person’s memory. It connects to the principle of Continuity and Transferability. When we describe a dish accurately, report a problem early and pass complete information to a colleague, we contribute to that continuity.

Belonging also grows from knowing that our contribution is needed and that we can improve together. Reporting a problem early must not itself become a reason for reprimand. Reporting allows action; it does not remove the obligation to act responsibly and correct the mistake.

#### The cultural foundation to develop over time

Italian cuisine includes different local traditions: regions, products, living conditions and knowledge passed down through families have helped shape them. Even simple ingredients gain value through knowing how to select, combine and prepare them. The Server needs to understand this before looking for elaborate words to sell a dish. Every presentation need not become a history lesson, and we do not invent recipe origins to make them interesting.

Authenticity involves concrete choices in ingredients, preparation and explanation. It does not mean declaring guests, their tastes or restaurants where they have worked inferior. We can explain that an offering has its own identity without claiming that people who prefer something else understand nothing. We encounter a real preference and look for an option compatible with who we are.

Tradition and habit need to be distinguished. We protect the heritage that gives the product meaning and value; we can improve tools, coordination and the way we explain it. “We have always done it this way” is not sufficient reason to preserve an organizational error. This does not authorize the Server to change a recipe: improvement follows the designated roles and discussion process.

The Brand lives in the expectation we create and its fulfillment. If we promise attentive service and then become unreachable, the promise weakens. If we describe a dish accurately and help someone choose what they were looking for, the promise becomes credible. Reputation develops through many consistent experiences and through how we handle mistakes.

Knowledge creates value when applied. Remembering an ingredient list is a beginning; understanding which difference helps that particular guest is the professional step. We do not measure the quality of an explanation by word count. Two relevant sentences may help more than a long description that does not listen to the person.

The team makes this application possible. A dish reaches the table through preparation, cleaning, available tools, information and coordination. A real need does not disappear because it arose outside our section: we can involve the right colleague or find help while maintaining the responsibilities and boundaries of our work. We do not abandon our tables without coordination, and we do not use the word team to expect one person to cover every gap alone.

Listening includes disagreement from guests. A complaint may indicate an error, an expectation that was not clarified, or a distance between preference and offering. Understanding it protects the relationship and provides information for improvement. Not every request is acceptable, and not every problem is solved by granting something: facts, respect and a decision at the proper authority level are required.

**Teaching instruction:** distribute this content across A1, A2 and later returns at subsequent levels. Do not deliver it as seven consecutive definitions. Start with the case closest to the person’s experience and keep the other connections for when they become concrete.

**Example to explain:** a guest asks for bacon in the Carbonara. Knowing the recipe allows us to explain that it already contains guanciale and find out what they actually want, without ordering an unauthorized modification. The connection to legacy is fidelity to the product together with care for the person.

**Possible first conversation:** “Let’s begin with where we work. Here our role is to help people discover a cuisine with its own identity. Then we’ll see how this idea becomes very concrete service. In our previous work, when did we feel we had really helped a table?” After the answer, connect a real element to the RF offering; do not automatically attribute enthusiasm.

**Complementary check, later:** a guest does not want a long explanation. Ask how to maintain RF identity in a short sentence and the service that follows. Expected response: adapt the length while retaining truth, help and reliability. Do not impose a lesson on the guest.

**Boundary:** Pino’s personal opening story remains a contribution to be agreed. Do not invent his words, events, motivations or an already available recording. Quoting the full legacy principles requires the approved text: this unit does not arbitrarily reconstruct their list.

### A2. The Server within the system

**Objective:** understand that guiding a table requires connection, precision and coordination.

The Server understands the need, guides the choice, records accurately and follows the experience. The Host welcomes and seats; the Server covers when the Host is unavailable. The kitchen prepares according to recipes and procedures. The lead handles decisions beyond the authority assigned. For every shift, we need to know who fills these roles and how to reach them.

We create value when the guest receives a suitable suggestion, the restaurant works with fewer errors and less waste, and the team can rely on the information it receives. A higher sale achieved by suggesting something unsuitable undermines this relationship. Failing to suggest a useful product can also mean incomplete service.

A reliable handoff contains the fact, reference and action needed: “Table 12, Pivot position 3: the guest reported a tree nut allergy. I have not confirmed any dish. Verification is needed before ordering.” The example table number does not define the local map.

**Exercise:** compare “The kitchen needs to pay more attention” with a report identifying the table, dish, problem and need. Have the person construct a useful handoff. Then return to the principle of continuity: a colleague must be able to act without starting from zero.

**When asked how we will be measured:** distinguish learning progress, practical verification and job performance. During the course we collect evidence to understand what to teach and practice. Leads decide readiness for independent work through practical observation. On the job, direct observation and KPIs interpreted in context can guide coaching. Pino has indicated, for example, tips per hour worked over a broad period. An index does not automatically become a pure measure of quality: it must be read in light of shift, section and conditions. Do not introduce rankings of absolute values or new, unagreed criteria. MyRF may help the person through targeted interventions where that function is active.

### A3. The table’s journey

**Objective:** build the map before the technical details.

Present the flow in four movements: we prepare; we understand and take orders; we serve and follow through; we conclude and prepare the table for the next guests. Then show the RF sequence in Appendix A without yet teaching every grip or phrase.

Three points must be clear immediately: the Host normally welcomes; we take appetizers and main courses in two visits; we always enter the order in the POS before preparing or serving. This last rule also applies to beverages, desserts and additions. Recording, sending and firing a course are distinct operations: details depend on the POS and local coordination.

**Case:** a table has chosen an appetizer but is still deciding on main courses. We enter the appetizer and allow the kitchen to begin; we return for the main courses. We do not wait for the complete order as a matter of principle. However, we do not promise exact synchronization that the kitchen has not confirmed.

**Check:** have the person identify the mistake in “I’ll bring two soft drinks now, then ring them in when I can.” Correct unambiguously: POS first. If the system is down, activate the approved procedure rather than improvising unrecorded service.

### A4. Reliability and basic safety

**Objective:** recognize boundaries before practicing with products, guests and equipment.

Teach these behaviors before any practical activity:

- verify before giving an uncertain answer; do not turn a memory into certainty;
- an allergy requires the procedure and verification with the kitchen: a menu description is insufficient;
- do not touch drinking rims, glass interiors, the working ends of utensils or food-contact surfaces;
- do not carry a load we cannot control; ask for help before losing control;
- report breakages, spills and hazards immediately, protecting the area according to procedure;
- in an emergency, activate the required assistance immediately rather than waiting for an administrative conversation or asking the AI for a diagnosis;
- do not grant modifications, compensation or exceptions beyond the authority actually assigned.

The course develops reasoning and service standards; local procedures for hygiene, allergens, emergencies and alcohol service must be supplied and taught before the related activities. The AI must not reconstruct them from generic knowledge.

**Example:** “I don’t see nuts in the description, so it is safe” is an incorrect answer. “Let’s verify ingredients and preparation with the kitchen before confirming” is the correct beginning; we must then actually follow the procedure and communicate the outcome.

**Moving to Level B:** the person can describe our contribution, recognizes the stages and does not suggest shortcuts around essential boundaries. Difficulty expressing themselves calls for a different check, not an automatic block.

## 6. Level B — Conducting a simple service

### B1. Knowing what we offer

**Objective:** turn reliable information into help with choosing.

For each product, teach a minimum card: name; category and role in the meal; defining ingredients; essential preparation; taste experience described without exaggeration; approved variations; information requiring verification. The sales description and allergen card serve different purposes.

Start with a few useful differences, then expand. Carbonara, Bolognese and Alfredo distinguish three identities: guanciale, eggs and Pecorino; meat ragù; a sauce based on dairy components. Do not ask the person to memorize the entire catalog in the first capsule. Use the historical repertoire in Appendix B for practice, then apply the method to the location’s current cards.

**Model explanation to a guest:** “The Carbonara has guanciale, eggs and Pecorino: it has a savory flavor. If we are looking for a milder dairy-based sauce instead, we can consider the Alfredo.” Wording must be adapted to the current recipe; it is not an allergen certification.

**Modifications:** we do not currently modify dishes freely. Variations that belong to a dish from its inception are intended offerings, not general permission. According to Pino’s clarification, the mushroom, bacon and truffle additions apply to Alfredo, not all pasta dishes. For salads, use the additions actually provided on the current menu. Future recipe revisions require the agreed company alignment; the Server does not anticipate them.

**Recurring questions:** for children, vegetarian, vegan or gluten-free requests, suggest only confirmed options. Egg pasta is not vegan; gnocchi are not automatically gluten-free. A preparation described as meat-free does not establish all the characteristics requested. Do not turn removal of an ingredient into a safety guarantee.

**Practice:** present two different preferences and ask for a reasoned suggestion for each. Then introduce a question for which information is missing: competence means recognizing the limit and checking, not completing the answer through invention.

### B2. Connecting and understanding

**Objective:** begin naturally and collect useful information without interrogating the table.

The RF communication method uses five actions: **We observe, We understand, We guide, We accompany, We remain available.** These are actions to recognize, not words to recite to the guest.

We observe facts: guests seeking eye contact, menus still open, an empty drink, a conversation in progress. We do not infer certain emotional states from posture, age, accent or clothing. We understand through a targeted question and listening to the answer.

For a first visit we might say: “Welcome. Here we focus on Italian cuisine rooted in tradition. I’ll be happy to help you find something you’ll enjoy.” A personal introduction can be brief and natural. If the guest already knows the restaurant, we do not automatically repeat the whole introduction. In a mixed group, include newcomers without making everyone listen to a long speech.

Start with a question that narrows the choice: “Are we looking for a milder flavor or something more pronounced?” If the answer is vague, ask for an example of a dish they enjoy. This is the method Pino described: a concrete example tells us more than a generic adjective. Do not accumulate five questions before offering help.

**Mistake to show:** suggesting the most expensive product before understanding the need. **Alternative:** use the answer to present one or two relevant options and explain the difference.

**Later check:** the guest replies, “I like everything.” Can the person offer a simple distinction or ask for an example without making the table do all the work of choosing?

#### Basic phrases for someone still developing independence

Teach a simple phrase for each function first. The person may adapt the wording when they preserve the meaning and demonstrate recognition of the situation. The phrases below are translations of the Italian models, to be adapted to the service language; they are not speeches to deliver all at the same table.

| When | Starting phrase | What must happen next |
|---|---|---|
| We do not yet know the preference | “Would you prefer a milder flavor or something more pronounced?” | Listen and use the answer in the suggestion. |
| The answer is too generic | “Could you give me an example of a dish you enjoy?” | Identify a useful characteristic without promising a copy. |
| We have two relevant options | “This one has these characteristics; the other goes in this direction.” | Help with comparison, then leave the choice to the guest. |
| We do not know a fact | “I’ll verify that before confirming it.” | Find the competent source and return with the outcome. |
| We need to approach | “Excuse me, I’ll place the plate here.” | Wait for space and use safe access. |
| An update is needed | “I’ll check the status of your dish and come back with an update.” | Check and actually return. |
| The guest declines the suggestion | “Of course.” | Continue with the same care, without insisting. |

Tone, volume and speed must support understanding without imposing our presence. Forced familiarity, physical contact and personal questions are unnecessary. When a phrase is insufficient, the beginner uses the boundary taught and asks for support; they are not invited to improvise an exception.

### B3. Guiding and taking the order

**Objective:** turn a guided choice into an accurate order that gets properly underway.

A useful suggestion connects what we have understood to a real characteristic: “If we are looking for something fresh and simple, we can start with the Caprese; if we prefer cured meats and cheeses, we can look at the board.” Avoid “Everything is delicious” as the only explanation, and do not make universal promises about taste.

Selling is part of guiding. We can suggest a dish to share, a relevant beverage, a paired wine or a dessert while explaining why it makes sense. If the guest declines, continue with the same care. Do not renew pressure using different wording.

The normal sequence is:

1. take and confirm appetizers, associating choices with Pivot positions;
2. enter the order correctly in the POS before beginning any preparation or service;
3. allow the necessary time to choose main courses and return for a second visit;
4. take, confirm and record main courses, coordinating the courses;
5. suggest choosing wine after the food so we can advise on pairing. If the guest already wants to order it, help without imposing an artificial delay.

If they do not want appetizers, move to main courses without staging an unnecessary visit. We do not wait until appetizers are finished to take main-course orders. The kitchen must receive correct, timely information; actual course timing must be coordinated.

Confirm names and quantities clearly. A similar name does not mean an equivalent dish. Essential requirements must not remain only in memory or an informal note. Do not promise precise times without reliable information.

**Practice:** a table chooses one appetizer to share, two different main courses and asks for wine. Have the person perform the steps in order and explain when to take the wine order. Add only one unexpected event: a main course is unavailable. The response must include a suitable alternative, renewed confirmation and an updated order.

### B4. Following through and concluding

**Objective:** make guests feel consistently attended to without interrupting them for automatic reassurance.

Remain visible, pass near the table and make contact easy. A discreet gesture may be enough when circumstances allow. An affirmative statement must refer to what we actually observe: do not say “I see everything is going well” in the face of contrary signs. We can offer a specific product or an allowed refill instead of constantly asking “Is everything wonderful?”

We do not adopt a mechanical two-minute check-back or five identical visits from Server and manager. This does not authorize ignoring an untouched plate, insistent eye contact or an obvious difficulty. In that case address the fact: “I see the plate is still untouched: is there something we need to address?” If a problem emerges, listen and act. The objective is real availability, not avoiding complaints.

Examples of specific attention: “May I refill the water?” or “We could offer an espresso with dessert.” Pricing, recording and refill rules are those currently in force; making an offer does not make it complimentary.

Clear with respect for anyone still eating. Present dessert and coffee through a specific suggestion, without imposing a sequence on someone who has expressed a preference. Prepare an accurate check and present it when requested. Continue attending to guests after payment. The table becomes available only after they actually leave, and it has been cleaned and reset.

**Check:** one guest wants to speak; another is telling the group a story. Present two separate situations and ask how to remain accessible without interrupting. Do not reward a standard phrase used without observation.

## 7. Level C — Executing precisely

### C1. Preparation, Pivot and coordination

**Objective:** eliminate avoidable mistakes before reaching the table.

Before service, check the assigned section, cleanliness and completeness, booth access, unavailable products, daily offerings, timing or issues communicated by the kitchen, and the shift lead. The detailed local checklist must be available; the AI does not replace an actual check with the person’s statement.

We use the **Pivot method**. The reference point and numbering direction must be identical for the order taker, POS and person delivering. The physical RF map must be attached: this document does not invent which seat is number one. Practice reading and reconstructing the map before using it with guests. Do not renumber people for convenience during the meal; any moves follow the local convention.

At the pass, check table, dish, position, completeness and relevant instructions. Do not take a dish merely because it resembles the expected one. Prepare the table before items requiring space or equipment arrive. If something is missing or a course risks interfering with the previous one, coordinate with the person responsible for the pass.

**Practice:** a correct order must be deliverable by a colleague. Have the person identify the information missing from “The two pastas are for the booth at the back.” Then practice the same delivery using the actual map when available.

**POS:** teaching buttons requires the current procedure. If older materials describe unconfirmed printouts, check marks or resending, do not reproduce them as current automatic steps. Recording before preparing/serving remains mandatory, with the designated procedure for any system outage.

### C2. Hands, glasses and the table

**Objective:** handle and place objects hygienically, under control and with respect for the guest’s space.

Hold glasses by the stem or lower outside section, not the drinking rim or interior. Touch utensils by the handle. Keep hands off the food-contact area of plates. Hands and equipment must follow the hygiene procedure and clean/dirty handling rules.

Place the glass at the upper right of the place setting, reachable and stable. With multiple glasses, water stays in the position closest to the guest specified by the RF setup; do not wedge objects together to reproduce an abstract arrangement. Demonstration at the actual table establishes the available space. Do not place a glass in the neighboring guest’s area for convenience.

If a phone occupies the necessary spot, ask: “Could we clear this space for the glass?” Prefer having the guest move personal belongings. Do not move phones, keys, bags, glasses, personal items or napkins in use without a relevant request or permission. Do not touch food, utensils or glasses in use to rearrange the setting at our own discretion.

For bottled beverages included in this type of service, present and open them at the table according to the product procedure. This does not automatically apply to fountain drinks, juices or wine by the glass. Pour with control, avoiding contact between the bottle opening and glass. Do not fill to the rim. Approved measures govern by-the-glass sales; the space left in a glass does not by itself determine the quantity sold.

A tray is useful only if we can use it. Do not teach a universal number of glasses held between the fingers as proof of skill. Maximum load depends on equipment, route and ability demonstrated in practice. Do not use a glass as a waste container or to scoop ice.

**Required practice:** picking up empty glasses, walking the route, placing them, handling an obstacle and controlled pouring. The lead observes stability and contact points. The AI can prepare for and discuss the test, not certify it from a self-description.

### C3. Plates, access and clearing

**Objective:** deliver and remove without endangering people, product or control of the load.

For plated service, the right side is preferred when it offers safe, natural access. In booths and semi-booths, accessibility and safety take priority: do not insist on an impossible side. Do not carry a plate across a guest’s face, pass a hot load over a person or ask someone to stand up to apply an abstract rule.

Before entering the necessary space, announce briefly: “Excuse me, I’ll place the plate here.” If safe access is unavailable, stop and ask for support. Do not move occupied tables or improvise booth maneuvers. The actual way to reach inside positions must be demonstrated for each table type.

Check the pass and route first. Carry a stable load that allows us to see where we are going. Use the designated dry protection for hot plates and warn the guest about the heat. More work does not automatically increase the number of plates we can carry.

Use Pivot positions to deliver the correct choice without routinely auctioning dishes. If reliable identification is missing, check before placing: do not guess. Coordinate deliveries to the same table and ask for help when the load requires it.

**RF clearing:** normally wait until everyone has finished the course, unless a guest requests otherwise or a need has been agreed. If unclear, ask permission with reference to the specific plate. Do not take away a plate while the person is still eating.

The non-dominant hand acts as a support on which to build a controlled load; the dominant hand collects. The maximum number is what has been demonstrated safe, not one number for everyone. Do not build stacks on the table or use the table as a station for rearranging the load. If we cannot control more material, make another trip or ask for help. The practical demonstration must distinguish clean from dirty surfaces and prevent drips or falling utensils.

Remove what is finished or unnecessary for the next stage. Retain water and wine still in use; check before taking an unfinished beverage away. Prepare utensils and space for the next course without invading the guests’ space.

**Progressive practice:** one cold plate; the authorized load; access to an unoccupied table; a booth with people playing guests; clearing without intermediate resting points; only then handling hot plates under supervision. A control error requires another demonstration and attempt, not encouragement to go faster.

### C4. Wine and beverages

**Objective:** advise using simple information and perform orderly, safe service.

The Server should not pretend to be a sommelier. They must be able to ask a preference, describe a few reliable differences and connect them to food and budget. Teach the necessary terms: sweetness, freshness/acidity, body, tannic sensation and aromas. A fruity wine is not necessarily sweet; body cannot be explained by alcohol content alone.

First ask what direction interests the guest and what food has been chosen; then suggest one or two bottles actually available, distinguishing them with a few accurate facts. Do not attribute universal characteristics to entire regions. Do not automatically suggest the most expensive bottle, and do not guarantee free tastes or opening bottles without the relevant policy.

For the chosen bottle:

1. check product, vintage where relevant, and condition according to procedure;
2. present the label and verify that it is the selection ordered;
3. open using the demonstrated technique, keeping tools and bottle under control;
4. offer the taste to the person who chose, according to the designated service;
5. after acceptance, serve guests using safe access and the table’s arrangements;
6. properly maintain the bottle, temperature and subsequent service.

Corkscrew and opening technique must be demonstrated physically. Do not use the cork’s smell as the sole test of wine quality. If a guest reports a defect, listen and involve the competent person; do not contradict them with an invented rule.

Serving order is not based on rigid classifications such as “always all women first.” Use the RF operating criterion, guest requests and accessibility, as demonstrated locally. Current procedures govern quantities, storage, wine by the glass and alcohol sales, including required checks and refusal of service when prescribed. The AI must not invent thresholds or acceptable documents.

**Practice:** the guest says, “I’d like a fruity red, but not a sweet one.” The person must understand the request, connect it to the food and verify a real option on the list without saying the two characteristics are mutually exclusive.

### C5. Communication in difficult situations

**Objective:** address the actual situation while maintaining responsibility, clarity and connection.

Apply the five RF actions: observe the fact; understand the need; guide toward a permitted solution; accompany the action; remain available until the outcome has been checked. Do not leave the guest responsible for chasing the problem.

| Situation | Standard action | Usable phrase | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product unavailable | Advise before the choice, offer a relevant alternative, confirm | “That is not available today. If we are looking for that kind of flavor, we can consider this option.” | Do not substitute without consent. |
| Delay | Verify status, give a real update, agree on the follow-through | “I’m checking your course with the kitchen; I’ll return with an update.” | Do not invent minutes or shift blame. |
| Wrong order | Identify table/dish, acknowledge the mistake, initiate correction | “A different dish from the one ordered has arrived. We’ll have it corrected.” | Compensation and priority follow assigned authority. |
| Dish not enjoyed | Listen to the concrete problem, clarify as needed, involve the lead | “I understand. Could you explain which aspect was different from what you expected?” | Do not promise a refund or criticize the guest’s taste. |
| Unapproved modification | Explain the boundary, understand the need, offer an allowed option | “We prepare this dish according to our recipe. Let’s look for an alternative closer to what you are looking for.” | Do not send a modification to the kitchen hoping it will be accepted. |
| Allergy | Stop confirmations, activate verification and procedure | “Thank you for telling us. Before confirming, we’ll verify with the kitchen.” | No guarantees based only on the menu. |
| Aggressive guest | Keep communication brief, involve the lead, protect safety | “I’ll involve the lead immediately to handle the situation.” | Do not manage a risky situation alone. |

An open question is appropriate when needed to understand a real problem. The rule against repetitive generic questions during the meal does not prohibit listening to a difficulty.

Guest compensation follows the current authorization level and must be recorded in the Compensation S-Domain according to the designated process. Automatic recording and authority to grant compensation are separate things. Do not introduce amounts or permissions from old manuals without confirmation.

**Check:** have the person handle a delay without certain information. The correct response includes seeking information and returning to the table; “it will arrive in two minutes” is incorrect if the time has not been verified. In the next case, change the context to an unauthorized recipe request. The person must recognize the different boundary.

## 8. Level D — Integrating and demonstrating

### D1. Complete service simulations

**Objective:** connect skills without turning every stage into a separate exercise.

Run four progressive scenarios. Give the person only the information they would actually have at that point. Do not introduce every problem together. Bring out plausible consequences: wrong recipient, longer wait, missing information, intrusion into personal space. Do not invent certain harm or dramatic reactions.

**Scenario 1 — The first simple table.** Two guests, first visit, no special requests. One prefers a savory sauce, the other a simpler dish. They choose an appetizer to share and two main courses. Practice opening, suggestions, two visits, Pivot, POS, table preparation, presence and conclusion. Check connections above all, not perfect vocabulary.

**Scenario 2 — The semi-booth.** Four guests, two on the booth side and two on chairs; a phone occupies a needed placement area. Practice access, permission, placement, course identification and clearing without stacks. The conversation prepares the test: the actions must be observed in person.

**Scenario 3 — Kitchen under pressure.** One table is ready for appetizers, a second requests the check, and a third seeks the Server’s eye contact. Ask for the next action and the necessary coordination. Do not impose an abstract priority ranking that applies everywhere: provide concrete signals, retain safety constraints and do not authorize bypassing the POS. Check whether the person reports overload early and arranges help.

**Scenario 4 — Continuity and changing colleagues.** The Server must hand a table over to a colleague. Appetizers, a wine and main courses have been ordered; a request still needs verification. The handoff must support continuity: table, positions, recorded orders, what has been served, what is missing, verified requirements, open points and who is handling them. Recall legacy here: we make the work transferable.

In every scenario, immediately interrupt a dangerous step or one based on an invented fact. Explain, show the alternative and have the person try again. For other mistakes, you may let a short sequence finish and then review it together. Do not let a wrong procedure become established to preserve the relaxed tone.

### D2. Practice, shadowing and transfer

**Objective:** verify on site what a conversation cannot demonstrate.

The lead organizes demonstration, guided practice, shadowing and observation. The amount depends on evidence; we do not automatically inherit the previous number of hours or days. AI conversation can replace part of the explanation and practice, while practical checks and the human decision about independence remain.

| Observed test | What must be visible |
|---|---|
| Preparation and Pivot | Station ready, map used correctly, unambiguous handoff. |
| Glasses and beverages | Correct grip points, stable load, controlled placement and pouring. |
| Plates and booths | Safe route and access, correct placement, timely request for help. |
| Clearing | Respect for the ongoing course, non-dominant hand as support, no stacks on the table. |
| POS and courses | Recording before service, correct positions and coordinated course delivery. |
| Connection and presence | Useful question, coherent suggestion, noticeable availability without serial interruptions. |
| Critical request | Correct handling of uncertain information, allergy or required authority. |
| Integrated service | Continuity from welcome to making the table ready again. |

Each test has a concrete outcome: demonstrated; repeat required with the error identified; not yet observed. The lead records context, support provided and operational limits. Do not use an average to offset a safety gap. Completing the conversation does not by itself authorize serving tables.

Leads reinforce the method during shifts through short real examples: an incident, the correct behavior and its connection to value created. Teaching their own staff also develops the ability to transfer the method. These training moments must be organized as working time according to the company process.

## 9. How to build coherent modules from this document

Each generated module must state: course ID and version; unit and level; observable objectives; prerequisites; authorized content; required local data; normal cases; permitted variations; mistakes to correct; conversational evidence; practical tests; connection to the next capsule.

A well-written objective is “Given an agreed appetizer order, record it before any service begins and return for main courses.” “Understand service” is not precise enough. The objective wording is for the Trainer; offer the participant a natural, brief explanation.

Do not add procedures to make the module richer. Examples may vary names, preferences and guest numbers while keeping rules intact. Do not invent a product fact to create a question. If an answer depends on missing local data, the exercise must teach how to verify it or be suspended only for that point.

### Example of a module that can be generated

**ID:** RF-SERVER-B3-ORDINE · **Content:** B3 and Appendix A, stages 3–7.  
**Prerequisites:** distinction between Host and Server roles; products in the example; POS rule; preliminary reading of Pivot.  
**Objective:** conduct two order-taking visits with confirmation, seat association and timely recording.  
**Introduction:** “Now let’s connect the choice to the kitchen’s work. Let’s see how to get the appetizer started while the table decides on the rest.”  
**Content to deliver:** first visit for appetizers, POS, second visit for main courses, POS and coordination; wine after food selection as a suggestion.  
**Normal case:** one appetizer to share and two main courses; nothing unavailable.  
**Mistake to introduce:** waiting for the complete order before sending everything.  
**Later variation:** the table does not want appetizers.  
**Complementary check:** a soft drink requested during the second visit; check that POS before service still applies.  
**Evidence:** correct sequence without a prompt on the essential step.  
**Separate practice:** execution in the actual POS using the current procedure.  
**Next step:** B4, following the table while work continues.

### Learning status and resuming

For each objective, distinguish: **introduced**, **understood with evidence**, **applied in simulation**, **demonstrated in practice**, **independent work authorized**. The last two require the designated observations and decision, not an AI inference. The summary for the person must make the distinction clear.

An effective restart says: “We connected choosing with ordering. In the simulation, we kept the POS step for the added beverage too. It still needs to be practiced on the real system. Today we’ll work on following the table without interrupting.” Do not say “Module passed” if requirements remain open that the message would imply were completed.

If the person asks a future technical question early, answer enough to help and indicate where we will explore it further. This preview does not automatically complete the later unit. If they demonstrate an existing competence, shorten the explanation and retain essential checks.

## 10. In-person use

The instructor uses the same order and objectives, adapting quantity and pace to the group. For each unit, choose a brief explanation, a demonstration or case, a practice attempt and a final recap. Alternate listening, a set table, menu, POS and simulation: do not turn the document into a continuous lecture.

Required materials: current menu, approved dish and allergen cards, current wine list, Pivot map, a regular table and a booth or semi-booth, glasses and plates, designated equipment, a practice POS environment and observation sheets. The introductory part may begin before all materials are ready; the related practical work may not.

Initial simulations use few products and few objects. First show a complete correct sequence, then break it down if needed. The person repeats it; the instructor corrects a concrete point and has them try again. Comparison with previous experience supports understanding, but does not prevent declaring an unsafe technique wrong.

Units do not equal days. The lead sets a schedule that allows information to settle and revisits concepts during shifts. Do not promise completion in three capsules or a fixed number of days.

## Appendix A — RF standard classic service flow

**Flow ID:** RF-SERVICE-STANDARD-CLASSIC · **Basis:** RF synthesis 0.2 of the decisions expressed. This course teaches the standard classic American service flow adapted to RF, booths and semi-booths. Other flows, including any smart service, require separate identity, version and assignment.

| Stage | What we do | Connection and check |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | Check section, tables, access, shift information and Pivot map. | A setting is not ready merely because it appears laid out. |
| 2. Welcome and seat | The Host welcomes and assigns. The Server covers when the Host is unavailable and receives useful information. | No information lost in the handoff. |
| 3. Open the relationship | Brief greeting, proportionate orientation, initial beverages and relevant requests. | Suggest wine after food for pairing; POS first for every order. |
| 4. Serve initial beverages | Verify product and recipient, carry and place safely. | Do not serve a beverage that has not been recorded. |
| 5. Take appetizers | First visit, confirmation and immediate POS entry. | The kitchen can begin while the table decides main courses. |
| 6. Take main courses | Second visit, confirmation, Pivot, POS and wine advice where relevant. | Do not wait for appetizers to finish; coordinate course delivery. |
| 7. Prepare and serve appetizers | Space and equipment ready, pass check, coordinated delivery. | Sharing plates and necessary utensils before the product. |
| 8. Follow the table | Presence, nearby passes, discreet contact and specific interventions. | No routine of repeated generic questions; attend to real signals. |
| 9. Clear and prepare | Normally after everyone has finished the course, unless requested otherwise or a need has been agreed. | Non-dominant hand as support; no stacks or load rearrangement on the table. |
| 10. Serve main courses | Pass check, table ready, correct recipients and access. | Continue following the table during the course. |
| 11. Guide dessert and coffee | Appropriate clearing, specific suggestion, order and POS before service. | Keep what is still in use. |
| 12. Prepare and present the check | Verify accuracy and present on request. | No pressure to leave the table. |
| 13. Complete payment and farewell | Verify the outcome through the designated system and maintain attention. | A closed check does not mean an available table. |
| 14. Reset | After departure: clear, clean, inspect seating, check for belongings left behind and set again. | Tell the Host when the table is actually ready. |

Stages 5–6 represent the normal order-taking sequence. If the appetizer is ready while the second visit is being completed, coordinate actions rather than leaving it at the pass to follow a table mechanically. The logic remains: timely start, accurate order, coordinated courses and attended guests.

## Appendix E — Progressive revision and quality control

Pino and Shelbi may modify content, examples and sequence while tracking version, reason and affected units. A revision proposal must state whether it changes a company rule, product information or teaching only. Another AI may suggest improvements, but cannot approve them on RF’s behalf.

Before publishing a module, check that it:

- teaches a concrete outcome and contains enough information to achieve it;
- respects prerequisites and recalls at least one useful connection to the pathway;
- does not contradict the flow, role boundaries or current cards;
- does not leave beginners decisions for which explicit criteria are missing;
- alternates explanation and practice without continuous interrogation;
- checks understanding through a case different from the sentence just explained;
- distinguishes conversational knowledge, practical demonstration and independence;
- produces a summary supporting a coherent restart;
- does not promise platform data, measurements or functions that do not yet exist.

The first pilot also serves to observe how many capsules the pathway requires, which explanations fail, and where better demonstrations are needed. Changes must improve understanding without lowering the final requirement or multiplying unnecessary content.

**Final criterion:** the person can connect the RF reason to the required behavior and demonstrate it with the designated support and checks. The course continues on the job through reminders and coaching, maintaining a recognizable, transferable common foundation.
