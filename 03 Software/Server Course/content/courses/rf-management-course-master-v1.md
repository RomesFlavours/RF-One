# The Management Course

Rome's Flavours. Version 0.2, 6 November 2026, built from *Management Training: the 4-Week Program, Draft 0.2*, approved in substance by Pino on 6 November 2026 ("start with it as it is"), aligned with Legacy 1.0 (revision 2), the Manager Model (Draft 0.2), Management Cross-Domain v0.3 and v0.4, and People v0.1.

## 1. What this course is

Four modules for the leads and managers: money and stock, whose decision it is, asking someone to do something, and owning the result. Each module has a case to reason through, a quick card, and one small real task in their own work. At the end, two private questions. There is no fixed pace: people who have more time go faster.

It is a separate course from the Server Course. It teaches how we reason as managers, using situations from restaurant work. It does not teach service.

The governing rule: **we are not rewarding the right answer; we are looking at the reasoning that leads to a sustainable conclusion.** A sustainable conclusion holds up: it starts from facts, considers who and what it affects, respects authority and limits, and says how we'll know it worked.

## 2. Instructions for the Trainer

You talk each situation through with one lead at a time. You don't test them and you don't grade them.

- Ask "why?", "what else would you check?", "who does this affect?" and "how would we know it worked?" until the reasoning is complete or the person has said what they can.
- Never reveal a model answer as "the right one" before they've reasoned. After they've reasoned, you can share the reference reasoning as one sustainable way of seeing it, and ask what they'd keep or change.
- "I don't know" and "I'd ask" are good answers when they're true. Ask who they'd ask.
- Asking for help, saying "I don't know", or admitting a mistake is never held against anyone. Raising a problem early is never held against anyone.
- Judge the reasoning by what they'd decide and why, not by how well they write or speak. Short answers in any language count the same as long ones.
- Separate facts from assumptions, and help them do the same.
- Label the layer when it matters: a Legacy principle (universal), a restaurant default (how most restaurants do it), or a Rome's Flavours configuration (our own limits, approvers and rules). Never present our configuration as a universal principle.
- Never invent our limits, amounts, approvers or procedures. If a detail isn't given here, say it comes from Pino or the procedure, and log it.
- When they describe how something actually works here, listen with interest. That's useful knowledge about how the work runs, and it's kept, without their name, to compare with the written procedures later. It is never treated as a complaint about anyone.
- Never use a real person's mistake as an example, and never name people. Roles, not names.
- Paid training time.

### The first session

Before the first case, in their language and in your own words, tell them:
- what the course is for: practicing how we reason as managers, with situations from restaurant work;
- that there's no score and no right answer to guess: we look at the reasoning;
- that their reasoning on each case is kept as training evidence, linked to the characteristics in the Manager Model, and kept separate from their real work;
- that anything they describe about how the work actually runs is kept without their name, to improve the processes;
- that nothing about health, family or private life is kept;
- that they can see what's recorded about them and correct it;
- that this is paid training time.

Then ask if it's OK to begin.

## 3. The tools from the Manager Model

**The four cards** (who decides):
- Green: it's mine, I decide.
- Yellow: it affects someone else, I consult them first.
- Blue: it's shared, we decide together.
- Red: it's on the mandatory list, it goes to Pino.

**The six questions** before spending: do we need it, how much do we have, how much do we use, when will we need it, can we move what we already own, is it the right priority?

**The assignment standard:** the outcome, what done looks like, the owner, the date, the checkpoint, and "how will you do it?"

**A problem or mistake:** facts first, then why. Check before, not after.

**The decision log:** who decided, when, the stage (thinking, proposal, decision), the expected outcome, who follows up, and later the actual result and the reasons for any difference.

**Agreed (Rome's Flavours configuration):** written approval for spending, stating what, model or size, quantity, price, what it's for and how much we already have. Nobody approves or checks their own spending.

**The seventeen characteristics** (Cross-Domain v0.3, 3.2), used to tag the reasoning:
1 Understanding the objective. 2 Accountability and reliability. 3 Technical competence. 4 Quality of reasoning and decisions. 5 Planning and prioritization. 6 Ability to deliver results. 7 Resource stewardship and economic value. 8 Quality and verification. 9 Communication. 10 Collaboration and delegation. 11 Transparency and respect. 12 Learning and development. 13 Self-management and readiness for work. 14 Managing emotional reactions and difficult situations. 15 Initiative and ownership. 16 Customer and reputation. 17 Safety and compliance.

## 4. The modules

### W1. Money and stock

Legacy 5, Evaluation: compare expected value, resources, risks and alternatives; a forecast is not proof of the outcome. Legacy 3: a low price and real value are different things.
Characteristics: 7, 4, 1. Method: the six questions, written approval, nobody approves or checks their own spending.

**The case.** A supplier has a great deal: 60 serving trays at half price. The restaurant uses about 5 a month, and there are already 20 in storage. 1. Do you buy them? Yes or no, and why. 2. Which of the six questions helps most here? 3. If someone else had already bought them, what would you do now?

What sustainable reasoning includes: starts from need, not price (20 in stock at 5 a month is four months; 60 more is twelve more months); sees the full cost (cash tied up, storage space, damage, what else that money could do now); checks the alternatives (the other location, buying when needed, a smaller quantity); on question 3, looks for what can still be done (return, move to the other location, pause future orders), records it, and doesn't look for someone to blame; knows it needs written approval and who approves.

**The card.** You're low on napkins. The other location has 10 cases it won't use for a month. Which card, and why?
Reference: yellow. The stock belongs to the other location, so you agree it with them before moving it. Moving what we already own comes before buying.

**The real task.** Pick one item in your area that you buy often. Count what's there, and write how many you use in a week. Then tell the Trainer what you found.

**Lesson.** Stock on a shelf is money that isn't working. Before buying, the question isn't "is it cheap?", it's "do we need it now, and what else could this money do?"

### W2. Whose decision is it?

Legacy, on authority: authority must exist and be clear, responsibility and power must match, who decides must be identifiable. Legacy 10, Time and Attention: escalating what's yours uses up someone else's decision-making capacity.
Characteristics: 4, 9, 10, 15. Method: the four cards; the decision log.

**The case.** Friday night, busy. The kitchen wants to stop taking orders for one dish because it's slowing the line. Front of house says guests have been asking for it all week. 1. Whose decision is it, and how do you know? 2. Who needs to be asked or told, and when? 3. What would you do in the next five minutes?

What sustainable reasoning includes: separates the facts (the line is slow, guests ask for the dish) from the assumptions (that stopping the dish fixes the line); names who owns the decision, and if it's shared between kitchen and front of house, decides together, quickly; considers the guest and the brand, not only speed (what the server says to a guest who asked for that dish); makes sure the decision reaches everyone affected (servers, host, the POS); thinks past tonight: if it might be recurring, it goes in the brief, not only in tonight's fix.

**The card.** A guest asks for a discount because the wait was long. It's within the limit. Which card, and why?
Reference: green for the front of house lead, because it's within their limit. It's still recorded, so a pattern of long waits can be seen.

**The real task.** Write down one decision you make at work: which card it was, and what you expected to happen. Then tell the Trainer, and later what actually happened.

**Lesson.** When it's yours, decide. When it touches someone else, ask first. When it's shared, decide together. When it's on the list, go to Pino. Sending Pino something that's yours is as much a mistake as not asking about something that isn't.

### W3. Asking someone to do something

Legacy 11, Continuity and Transferability: what we pass on is the ability to decide, not only the instruction. Legacy 17, People: people are sources of knowledge and judgment, not task performers.
Characteristics: 10, 5, 1, 9. Method: the assignment standard; agreed commitment, work performed, deliverable produced, outcome verified.

**The case.** You ask a team member to "clean up the storage room before Saturday." On Saturday it's tidier, but the boxes are blocking the shelves you needed. 1. What went wrong, and whose part was it? 2. What should you have said when you asked? 3. What could you have asked or checked to catch it early?

What sustainable reasoning includes: sees that the work was done but the outcome wasn't reached, and that those are different; takes their own part (the request didn't say the result, or why); rewrites the request with the result, what done looks like and the date; puts in a checkpoint before Saturday and asks "how will you do it?" so the plan comes out early; treats the team member as someone who can judge, once they know what it's for.

**The card.** You gave someone a task due Friday. On Wednesday they tell you they're stuck. Good news or bad news, and why?
Reference: good news. A problem raised before the deadline can still be solved. Raising it early is never held against anyone.

**The real task.** The next time you give someone a task, say the result you want, the date and why it matters, and ask "how will you do it?" Then tell the Trainer what happened.

**Lesson.** Telling someone to do something isn't delegating. Say what done looks like and why, check they have what they need, ask how they'll do it, and agree when you'll check. You still own the result.

### W4. Owning the result

Legacy 9, Recurrence and Reusability: recurrence signals a possible structural cause and an opportunity for learning. Legacy 8: case, understanding, criterion, new application, verification. Legacy 19: verification compares objectives, hypotheses and results.
Characteristics: 2, 8, 11, 12. Method: possible structural cause, learning, new application, verification. Facts first, then why. Delays are attributed to their real cause.

**The case.** A delivery was short two cases. Nobody checked it when it arrived. You only find out on Saturday, when you run out. 1. What happened, and then why? Is this the first time something like this has happened? 2. What in the way receiving works made it possible: who receives, what they check against, and when? 3. What should change to reduce or prevent recurrence, and how will we know whether it worked?

What sustainable reasoning includes: starts with the facts, without blame, and asks whether it has happened before; looks for a structural cause in how the work is organized (no check against the order and invoice, no clear owner for receiving, deliveries arriving when nobody is free), not only "someone forgot"; owns their part as the person responsible for the area, even if someone else received it; proposes a change to the process, not only "be more careful"; says how we'll know it worked, with something observable over a set period (for example, no short deliveries signed for in the next four weeks, or every delivery checked against the invoice); sees that the change has to be agreed by whoever owns the process, and checked after it's in place.

**The card.** Something in your area went wrong because of a step you didn't check. Do you say "nobody told me" or "I should have checked"? And what's the next question?
Reference: "I should have checked." Then: "What in how we work let it happen?" Owning it is how it gets fixed, and nobody gets blamed for owning it.

**The real task.** Pick one thing that didn't go as planned recently. Tell the Trainer four things: what happened, the possible structural cause, what should change, and how you'd know it worked.

**Lesson.** Facts first, then why. When something happens more than once, look at how the work is organized, not only at who was there. Change the process, then check that it worked. A manager owns the result, even of work they delegated.

### W5. Looking back

Legacy 19: verification and learning, including of the course itself.
Characteristics: 12.

Two private questions, after the four modules: what got better in how I work, and what two things I want to work on next. Then one question about the course: which case was the most realistic, and which one should change?

## 5. What is kept

- **About the person (for People):** their reasoning on each case and card is training and simulation evidence, never "demonstrated in practice". What they report about their real task is self-reported real work, not verified. What they say at the end is their own account. No score, no ranking.
- **About how the work runs (for Organization):** what they describe about how something actually works here is current reality, to verify. It never becomes an approved procedure on its own.
- **Never kept:** health, alcohol, family, private life, immigration status, or anything inferred about them.
