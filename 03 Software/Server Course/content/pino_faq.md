# FAQ from diners

Pino's list of the questions guests ask most (January 2024, received 3 November 2026), lightly edited for clear English. Where it differs from the Trainer manual's company answers (section 4), section 4 is newer and wins; the differences are noted in brackets.

## 1 Requests for things we don't do

- Can I change toppings on a pizza? Some changes are possible, but the kitchen has to confirm them. [The manual's current rule is no modifications to dishes for now. Don't promise any change: for a preference, explain the dish and suggest a closer one; for an allergy, follow the allergy rule.]
- Can I change other dishes? Our recipes are the traditional ones, some of them centuries old. It isn't a good idea to change something perfected over centuries.
- Do you have a kids' menu? Unfortunately not, but we have kid-friendly dishes: cheese pizza, butter and Parmigiano pasta, olive oil and Parmigiano pasta, Alfredo, Napoli, and a side of meatballs. If there's more than one child they can share, or take what's left home in a box.
- Do you have chicken penne, chicken parmigiana, spaghetti and meatballs? This is an authentic Italian restaurant inspired by the oldest, most traditional cooking of Italian grandmothers. Those dishes are American interpretations of Italian cuisine.
- Do you serve sides of pasta? No, but you can order a pasta dish from the menu and add a protein dish.
- Can I have a different pasta with a certain sauce? Each pasta and sauce is matched to be the best pairing, so we recommend keeping it. If a guest really hates a pasta shape, it can be done. The one excellent swap is tagliolini instead of gnocchi with the pesto. [Under the current no-modifications rule, don't offer this; if it comes up, check with the front of house lead.]
- Can I have some bread? Yes, if asked. But we don't offer bread; rather avoid giving it. Our bread is homemade and we were throwing away huge quantities.

## 2 The main ingredients

- Fettuccine, tagliolini, pappardelle, angel hair: the difference is the width. Angel hair is very thin. Tagliolini are similar to linguine. Fettuccine are a little wider. Pappardelle are like a wide ribbon.
- Is the homemade pasta vegan? No: it's made with eggs (and flours, olive oil and apple vinegar; that's the whole recipe, but you don't need to mention it). We have a dry pasta that is vegan and very good, but it isn't made in house, takes longer (about 20 minutes) and is NOT gluten free. [Pino's notes say the egg pasta is "neither vegetarian nor vegan". Pasta with egg is normally considered vegetarian; to confirm with Pino. Until then say it's made with eggs and isn't vegan.]
- I'm celiac, do you have gluten-free pasta or pizza? Gluten-free pasta yes, pizza no. But if the allergy or intolerance is severe there may be a problem: we make the pizza, pasta and gnocchi dough here, using flour many times a day, so flour is everywhere in the kitchen. Tell the kitchen every time.
- Gnocchi: made here from freshly made dough with potatoes and flour, each piece cut by hand into roundish pieces, similar to dumplings. They are vegan but NOT gluten free.
- Basil pesto (allergy alert: nuts and dairy): a sauce made here from crushed basil leaves, extra virgin olive oil, Grana Padano and pine nuts, blended into a creamy green sauce. Can you take the pine nuts out? No: the sauce is made fresh every morning, not to order. All the ingredients are blended together and left to sit so the sauce is full of flavor. [The menu's gnocchi card lists Parmigiano Reggiano in the pesto: which cheese, to confirm with the kitchen.]
- Our pizza dough: made fresh here with our own recipe. There are many kinds of dough; ours uses natural yeast, not artificial, water imported from Italy (San Benedetto, if they ask the brand) and a blend of Italian flours. In Italy, and in Rome, there isn't one kind of pizza but many, depending on the dough (thin and crusty, thin but softer, very soft and so on).
- Is the pizza dough vegan? Yes, there are no animal products. [But every pizza on the menu has mozzarella, and both gnocchi dishes have cheese: no pizza or gnocchi dish is vegan as it is. Tell a vegan guest honestly; what can be offered is for the front of house lead and the kitchen, to confirm with Pino.] Water is an essential ingredient of pizza: New York pizza is famous because of its water, and the same recipe with another water wouldn't come out the same.
- Is the pizza crusty? Crusty on the bottom and a little soft on top, not very thin. If a guest likes it thin, the kitchen can make it a bit thinner and crustier, as far as the dough allows, but the recipe isn't meant for a thin, crusty pizza. [A change to the dish: under the current no-modifications rule, don't offer it.]
- Gluten-free pizza? No. It exists, we just don't know if any restaurant in the area serves it.
- Lasagna (allergy alert: nuts, dairy and pork): the traditional Bolognese lasagna, layers of pasta sheets with Bolognese sauce, Grana, mozzarella and bechamel. With a nut allergy, lasagna is not the right dish: there's a touch of pesto on top, hard to taste. Is there pork? Yes, a little ground pork cooked with the ground beef in the Bolognese. Can you take out the pesto, or anything else? No: the lasagna is made ahead in a big pan and has to sit for many hours (straight out of the oven it would be too liquid), so no ingredient can be removed. [Lasagna isn't on the current Winter Park menu; this applies when it's served.]
- Regular mozzarella or buffalo mozzarella? Buffalo mozzarella is made from buffalo milk. It's milkier and creamier (when you cut it, more milk comes out), with more flavor, a little saltier, and softer.
- Grana Padano: a cheese very similar to Parmigiano, aged a little less and made in a slightly different way. Grana is a protected (DOP) cheese made in some regions of northern Italy; Parmigiano is made around Parma, in Emilia Romagna, which gives it its name. They taste very similar. If a guest asks for more Parmigiano, bring them more of our grated cheese. [Pino's notes say not to mention it's Grana. The manual says describe truthfully: if the guest asks what it is, say it's Grana Padano, very close to Parmigiano. To confirm with Pino.]
- Prosciutto: cured pork, charcuterie. Not raw: cured, meaning treated with salt and other ingredients and spices that make it ready to eat. We buy the whole leg and slice it fresh to order (there's a picture on the wall you can point to).
- Prosciutto di Parma: prosciutto made in Parma, in Emilia Romagna, the Italian region most famous for foods known around the world, like Parmigiano, prosciutto, tortellini and lasagna. It's a DOP product: its production is protected and it can only be made in that area.
- Salame or pepperoni: salame isn't one specific cut of the pork like prosciutto. It's usually made of different parts, fat and lean, ground together, typically round.
- Meatballs (allergy alert: dairy): ground beef with a touch of ground pork, with dairy, some bread, parsley and garlic. [The menu's components also list pesto and bacon. Pesto contains pine nuts and cheese: treat the meatballs as containing nuts and dairy until the kitchen confirms.]

## 3 Desserts

- Tiramisu: a layered creamy cake. The cream is whipped heavy cream, mascarpone, amaretto and marsala, between layers of ladyfingers soaked in coffee, with cocoa powder on top. [Amaretto is an almond-flavored liqueur and marsala a wine: the dessert contains alcohol, and with a nut allergy the kitchen must confirm. The ladyfingers contain wheat and eggs.]
- Pannacotta: like a flan custard, made with heavy cream, creamy, soft and very delicate. Its absolutely smooth texture is its main feature. Topped with Nutella, strawberry, blueberry, or espresso (affogato).
- Sicilian cannoli: crunchy shells filled with a ricotta cream, with caramelized pistachios. They're typical of Sicily, Italy's island at the bottom (the triangle one).
- Campo de' Fiori: a dessert for the table, for two to four guests. A personal-size pizza dough baked in the oven, then topped with Nutella (the very popular Italian cream of cocoa and hazelnut) and powdered sugar. [The menu says a slice of focaccia; which dough, to confirm with Pino.] It's named after a square in Rome where young people go out at night. [Nutella contains hazelnuts and milk.]
