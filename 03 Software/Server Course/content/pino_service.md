# The Service at Rome's Flavours

Pino's service course (written 2023 to 2025, received 3 November 2026). Lightly edited for clear English; the rules are his. Where it differs from the Trainer manual's company answers (section 4), section 4 is newer and wins.

We use the Italian service style. It is the most common one and similar to the US style; this course covers where they differ. Serving a table correctly comes from good training and preparation, so you know every step of service. Use common sense only where the Italian standard can't be applied.

The phases of service: 1. Welcome and restaurant presentation (welcome, presenting yourself, presenting the menu). 2. Taking orders (water and drinks, dishes, wine, the pivot). 3. Serving drinks and food (serving, refilling, bussing). 4. Control of your tables. 5. Service speed. 6. Working with the host. 7. The check, thanks and Arrivederci.

## 1 Welcome and restaurant presentation

The first goal is to understand whether your table is new guests, returning guests or a mix.

Never use the US form "Good evening, my name is John Smith, I will be your server" (it says nothing).

New or unknown guests: welcome them, "Good morning / Good evening, welcome to Rome's Flavours". If you don't know them, find out whether they've eaten with us before, because the service is different for new guests: "I don't think I've served you before. Has someone on our team?"

Verified new guests need special attention, to turn them into regular guests (or at least get a good review):

- Introduce the restaurant. Fix a few ideas in their mind: like in an Italian home, traditional recipes, fresh food made from basic ingredients; we are truly authentic, not like the others they've had. Like in Italian homes, the flavors reflect ancient Italian tradition; the food is always fresh, chosen to be prepared and enjoyed right away. No tricks to meet common taste: there's no other way to be authentic.
- Introduce yourself. The goal is a personal relationship with the guests, win-win, offering to help them choose what they'll like most: "My name is John. It will be a pleasure to help you choose what best suits your taste among what Rome's Flavours can offer you."
- Present the menu: (1) we cook from scratch, so dishes take the time they need; (2) we use mainly products imported from Italy; (3) the areas of the menu and their main features:
  - Chef's signature dishes.
  - Pasta, made in house: fresh eggs from cage-free hens, durum and soft wheat, extra virgin olive oil, a touch of vinegar. Sauces: tomato based (Napoli, Norma, Puttanesca, Arrabbiata), cheese based (Alfredo, Cacio e Pepe), meat based (Bolognese, Amatriciana), fish based (Shrimp and Pistachio, Colatura di Cetara), vegetable based.
  - Pizza dough: three different Italian flours, to make it crunchy, soft and tasty at the same time; water imported from Italy; natural yeast with a 24-hour rise.
  - Meat and fish: a choice of fish, lamb, beef and chicken.
- Present the wine (new guests only): only Italian wine, imported, from small winemakers.

Mixed table: use the new-guest presentation, addressed only to the new guests.

Returning guests: welcome them back in a warmer, welcoming tone, without being over-familiar: "Welcome back to Rome's Flavours, it's a pleasure to serve you again." Don't hug returning guests: other guests may think there's favoritism. Stay professional at all times.

## 2 Taking the order

Writing it on your pad: memorize the spot you approached the table from. Write numbers for the guests in order from there, well spaced (the pivot). Write each guest's order next to their number. Then go straight to the POS without doing anything else.

At the POS: always sign the previous person out and sign yourself in, without exception. Always go back to the home screen. Make sure you open the right table. Set the number of guests to your actual guests.

Entering items:

- Drinks and wine: under the right guest. A bottle of wine goes under the table; if a guest is offering the bottle, under that guest. Never prepare a drink that hasn't been entered.
- Starters: under the guest, or under the table if shared. Enter changes with the modifiers, never by hand. Add the modifier "First" immediately, or you may forget it when you enter the mains. Print strictly in this order: first for the kitchen, with drinks and wine unchecked; then for the front of house bar, with all items selected. Hang it on the right bar.
- Mains: enter each item under the guest's number, with modifiers for any change. Print the whole order, starters included, strictly in this order: first for the kitchen (drinks and wine unchecked), then for the front of house bar (all items). If the starters were already served, mark them with a marker. If they weren't, tell the kitchen to switch the order for that table. Hang it on the right bar.
- Desserts: enter them under the guest's number. Print first for the kitchen, then for the front of house.

The check: always check that every item served was entered before printing it. When you think the table won't order anything else, print it and keep it on the counter, so you're quick when they ask.

Payment: when the guest puts down their card or cash, take it, thank them and go back to the POS with it in your hand. Process the payment, then release the table. Put the signed check and your cash tips in your own bank bag. For no reason can you have cash or checks in your pockets: it's a serious breach of the written policy (you could be fired for it).

## 3 How to serve

Glasses: above the plate, slightly to the right. The water glass is closest to the plate, then the wine glasses. If that space is taken, use the nearest position, always to the right of the plate, without invading the next guest's space; you can politely ask permission to move an object, or work with the team to clear space. If there's no water glass, any other drink goes in front of the right corner of the plate.

Drinks: hold every stemmed glass (wine, water, other drinks) by the stem, so you can only serve two at a time. Hold a glass without a stem as low as possible. Empty stemmed glasses can be carried upside down between the fingers of the left hand, then placed in front of each guest. Every drink is uncorked or opened at the table, except fountain sodas, juices and wine by the glass. Pour toward 10 to 12 o'clock (12 to 2 with the left hand), label visible; never pour toward the outside of your arm. To refill water, take the glass by the stem, pour smoothly and put it back in front of the right corner of the plate. Refill from bottles already on the table without touching the glasses. Water: leave a quarter of the glass empty. Wine refills: only up to a third of the glass.

Plates: preferably two at a time; only in a rush up to three (two on the left arm, one in the right hand). Hold them firmly, palm up, forearm parallel to the floor, at least 8 inches (about a hand's width) from your body. Thumb along the edge of the plate, not across it. Lift the plate just above the highest object on the table, never at the height of the guest's face.

Service rules: always announce the name of the dish. Serve from the guest's right, clear from the left, walking around the table to reach each guest from the correct side. If serving from the right is impossible, serve from the left, never reaching across the table. Right hand when serving from the right, left hand if you have to serve from the left.

Order of service: children first, then ladies from the oldest, then men from the oldest.

Table preparation: always set the table for the next course before serving it. Never serve food or drinks if the table hasn't been cleared from the previous course or if there isn't enough space in front of each guest. If needed, take the dishes back to set the table, or ask for help while holding them. For shared dishes, bring empty plates before serving.

Watching the room: full attention, no distractions or personal conversations. Watch the whole room, not just your tables, and notice any sign a guest needs something without waiting to be called. Silverware always present and perfect on every table; glasses always filled without the guest asking. Anticipate needs: refills, condiments, clearing empty plates. A natural smile and a friendly presence. Attention to detail: remove empty plates or broken glass immediately, and handle any issue discreetly. Clear, constant communication with the team. Speed and accuracy in every action.

Bussing during service: don't remove plates until everyone at the table has finished, unless a guest asks, so nobody feels rushed. Watch the pace discreetly; if a plate has sat untouched for a long time, politely ask if you can take it. Exceptions: very fast service or informal settings, where plates are cleared as each guest finishes.

Clearing: never sit or put a knee on a booth to reach something. The time to clear is when all guests have finished, unless one asks. Always ask before removing a plate. Remove plates one at a time; never stack dishes on the table before picking them up. Stack up to three dishes on the left arm. In a booth, clear the farthest dishes first. Whenever possible, clear from the guest's left with the left arm. Before dessert, clear all dishes and silverware. Right hand to clear from the right, left hand from the left.

Tableware: never use a glass as a trash can for paper or anything else. Never hold a glass by the rim when clearing. Never touch silverware by the part that was in the guest's mouth. Silverware, folded or not, is brought on a service plate. Napkins are never removed from the table.

## 4 Opening a bottle at the table

Serving wine is a ritual with codified rules. It pleases the guest, makes them more aware of the wine and gives a great tasting experience. Explain the wine's fundamental characteristics; complex technical explanations are useless.

1. Take the bottle from the fridge, clean it, and bring it to the table without shaking it.
2. Show the closed bottle's label, saying the name first and then the vintage, if the bottle has one.
3. Holding the bottle with your other hand, remove the capsule with the corkscrew knife: cut it near the mouth of the bottle and put it in your pocket.
4. Close the knife and open the spiral. Put the tip in the center of the cork, holding the bottle firmly, and make half a turn to set it.
5. Screw the spiral in clockwise, stopping before the last turn so you don't pierce the whole cork.
6. Put the first step of the lever on the rim and pry the cork out about half an inch, holding lever and neck tightly. Then use the second step to finish pulling it out.
7. Smell the end of the cork that was inside the bottle. If it smells of vinegar, apologize and get another bottle.
8. Take a tasting glass by the stem, without leaving fingerprints, pour about a quarter inch for the person who ordered the wine to taste, and set it on the table.
9. After a positive opinion, pour for everyone drinking that wine without touching the glasses [how much to pour: the original gives different amounts, to confirm with Pino]. By etiquette, women are served first, then men, from the oldest; or start from the right. Go to each guest's right and use your right hand, palm open toward the guest, label showing as much as possible.
10. Never let drops fall on the table. Either dry the neck with a service napkin (paper is fine) in your left hand, or, as you finish pouring, lift the neck above the glass while turning the bottle counterclockwise.

## 5 Control of your tables

In many restaurants a server looks after only their own tables. At Rome's Flavours, teamwork gets much better results than the sum of individual servers, so every server keeps the whole dining room under control, not just their tables.

Position: be physically where you can see the most tables and guests, as far as the task you're doing allows. Nobody stays in the back except for operational tasks.

The radar: your eyes scan every part of the room like a radar, even while folding silverware, looking for signals that need you. The main things to watch:

- The podium and the door. A guest approaching the restaurant must be taken care of right away by someone. It's the host's first priority, but if the host isn't there or is busy, take charge of that guest immediately, whatever you're doing.
- The food pass. As soon as a plate appears in the kitchen window, every server, as far as their task allows, takes it out of the window onto the counter, checks which order it belongs to and alerts the table's server, or serves it themself if that isn't possible. Food is served as quickly as possible, even interrupting another task.
- Tables and guests. Look at the empty tables, to make sure they're set and ready, and at the occupied ones (everyone's, not just yours). If an empty table isn't ready, act or ask someone to. If you notice even a glance asking for something at any table, tell that table's server or act yourself.

Your own tables are your responsibility. At every moment, be sure the guest needs nothing that isn't already on the table: check the table setting for each phase of service; anticipate what the guest is likely to need at that stage; catch the guests' eyes by passing near your tables often, without interacting just to ask if they want something (which interrupts them and invites requests); and step in early: if a table has waited a while and has gone quiet, or shows signs of impatience, ask the kitchen for the timing, then go to the table to apologize, explain and reassure them it's coming shortly.

## 6 Service speed

Service time is flexible, to fill the restaurant as much as possible, care for guests, and send the kitchen as even a flow of orders as possible.

When to change speed:

- Too many orders on the order bar: slow down your order-taking so the kitchen can keep up.
- Few tables occupied: slow down, so the restaurant doesn't look empty.
- Few orders on the bar at peak time: speed up order-taking, to avoid later spikes for the kitchen.
- Most tables occupied and few orders on the bar: speed up, to turn tables over.
- You have many tables and a normal flow on the bar: speed up, to reduce your time at each table.
- Another reservation for the same table right after or overlapping: speed up that table.

How to speed up (adapt to the table): talk less about things not related to service; if your other tables allow it, talk with guests only while they wait for food; take drinks and starters together; take the mains right after serving the drinks, without leaving the table; check they're enjoying the food while pre-bussing; leave the dessert menu when you pre-bus the main course; print the check right after serving desserts and keep it ready. Only if the table must be freed urgently, pre-bus the dessert plates and leave the check on the table saying "No rush."

How to slow down: talk longer at the start about the restaurant, the food, the owners, as far as your other tables allow; take orders one course at a time (drinks, starter, main, dessert, coffee).

## 7 Working with the host

The host's goals, in priority order: (1) hold the podium and draw in guests; (2) welcome guests, update OpenTable, seat them; (3) set the tables; (4) help servers get their tables ready for new guests; (5) help servers with other things, on request. All of this makes your work easier, improves service, and increases the restaurant's revenue and your tips.

Ask the host for help as little as possible, so you don't get in the way of their first priority. When the host isn't where these tasks happen, they're your job:

1. Hold the podium and draw in guests: guard the podium and talk to people reading the menu outside to get them to sit down. Coordinate with the other servers so the podium is never unattended.
2. Welcome and seat guests: welcome guests and walk-ins, update OpenTable immediately to avoid double-booking a table, walk them to the table, bring the menus and check there's silverware (if not, bring it first).
3. Reset your tables: you're responsible for clearing and setting the table you just served. The host helps as much as possible, but it's your job to make that possible and get your table reset quickly.
4. Asking the host for help: you can, as a procedure, but the host must always stay able to do their priorities. If the task would keep the host in the back for more than a few seconds, you must make sure you can cover the host's tasks yourself. The host can tell you a task would stop them following their priorities.

## 8 The check, thanks and Arrivederci

Prepare the check as soon as it seems the table won't order anything else, and keep it ready. Bring it only when the guest asks, never before. Once it's on the table, step away right away; don't stand next to it waiting for payment. As soon as the guest puts down their card, take the check.

When guests leave, greet and thank them. Use the Italian word "Arrivederci", which means see you again.
