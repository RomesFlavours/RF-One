# Wines

Pino's wine course (received 3 November 2026), lightly edited for clear English. Two historical dates in the original are corrected here (noted in brackets). How to open a bottle at the table is in the service course.

## What wine is

Wine is the product of the fermentation of the sugars in grapes. More precisely, it's an alcoholic drink made when yeasts (the saccharomyces family) turn the sugars in the grapes into alcohol.

## The basic winemaking process

Winemaking goes through successive phases. At each one the winemaker makes choices that depend on the grapes harvested and the wine they want to make.

- The harvest. Grapes are picked depending on how ripe they are, which varies by grape variety and the wine wanted. The longer grapes stay on the vine, the more sugar and color they have and the less acidity. White wines meant to be drunk young and fresh are harvested rather early; wines meant for aging stay on the vine longer, until the grapes are fully ripe.
- Destemming and crushing (extracting the juice). The berries are separated from the stems, which contain a lot of tannin that would spoil the wine. Crushing breaks the berries so the pulp comes out of the skin.
- Alcoholic fermentation. This is winemaking itself: yeasts turn the sugars (fructose and glucose) in the juice into alcohol.
- Refining and aging. After fermentation the wine is racked and filtered, then left to settle and age. In inert containers (steel, cement, fiberglass) this lasts from a few weeks to a few months. Maturing in large wooden barrels or small ones (barriques and tonneaux) lasts from several months to several years.
- Bottling. After maturing, the wine is racked and filtered again and bottled. It then needs to settle again, so it usually rests in the bottle one to three months before going on sale.

## Italian wines

We only serve Italian wine. The restaurant's goal is to offer authentic Italian products, so the wines are only Italian too.

Wine varieties in Italy: no other country comes close to Italy's number of grape varieties. Almost every region (roughly like a large county) has many, some a dozen or more. The reasons:

- Climate: the north, center and south have very different climates, so only the most cold-resistant vines spread to the north, and only the most heat-resistant to the south.
- History: from the end of the Western Roman Empire [476 AD; the original says 456 BC] until unification [1861; the original says 1851], Italy was split into many states, sometimes city states, which stopped goods, including vines, moving between them.
- Soil: the soil changes constantly (rocky, sedimentary, volcanic), so some vines take root well in one area and hardly at all in another.

General character: Italian grapes are all very rich, but in the south, where the sun and good summer weather last two or three months longer than in the north, grapes are richer in sugar and flavor. So southern wines generally reach more body and alcohol without special work in the cellar, while northern wines are fermented further to reach a higher alcohol level, leaving less residual sugar, so they're drier. Amarone is the classic exception: from the northeast, yet high in alcohol and body, because the grapes are left to dry on mats for two or three months, losing water and concentrating sugars and flavor.

The general rule: southern wines are generally more natural and simple, northern ones more complex and sophisticated.

- Wines of the north: dry, light-bodied (except Barolo/Nebbiolo and Amarone), complex and sophisticated.
- Wines of the center: medium-bodied, clear but rich in nuances. [The original says "sweet"; central Italian reds such as Chianti are dry wines, so "sweet" here likely means soft or round. To confirm with Pino.]
- Wines of the south: fruity, full-bodied, clean, with hints of berries.

## We buy from small direct importers

This gives us: a more careful selection of wines from the supplier; "exclusive" wines, not available through the big distributors to restaurants and stores; mid-range wines that are better and less known, which other restaurants don't carry because they choose only the best-known, easiest-to-sell wines; and a lower buying cost, so a lower price for the guest.

## How to suggest wines

To understand what the guest likes, ask:

- Do you like red or white?
- If red: do you like it dry or more fruity? Light, medium or full-bodied?
- If white: do you like it sweeter or more acidic?

This lets you suggest a group of two or three wines.

Samples: keep in mind which wines are open in the fridge. When you have time to take more care of a table, you can give samples ONLY of wines already open. You may open ONLY one bottle to give a sample, and ONLY if the table is asking for a whole bottle. In that case bring the flier with the other samples and an empty shot glass, and open the bottle at the table. Nine times out of ten they'll take that bottle, and you can leave it on the table.
